      the Big Bang.

Neutrinos are particles with no electric charge and almost no mass. They pass through nearly everything. Trillions go through your body every second, most of them from the Sun, and you never know it. That makes them superb messengers and very hard to catch. A neutrino made beside a black hole in another galaxy will cross the universe in a straight line and then cross the Earth as well.

Francis Halzen’s idea was to use a detector so large that a few would be caught anyway, and to let nature supply it. Deep Antarctic ice is dark and extremely clear. On the rare occasion that a neutrino strikes an atom in the ice, the collision throws off a charged particle that leaves a faint trail of blue light. IceCube is 5,160 light sensors on 86 cables, lowered into holes melted with hot water and then frozen in place. Together they watch a cubic kilometre of ice.

In 2013 the collaboration reported the first high-energy neutrinos known to come from beyond the solar system. Since then IceCube has traced neutrinos to a distant galaxy with a giant black hole at its centre, to a second active galaxy 47 million light-years away, and to our own Milky Way. This starts to answer a question that has been open for more than a century: where cosmic rays, the most energetic particles ever observed, come from.
