    what: >-

      family moves round small-town Ontario as he is posted. She takes three
      degrees in classics at the University of Toronto, and a diploma in
      classics from St Andrews in Scotland. Her doctoral thesis, *Odi et Amo
      Ergo Sum*, is published in 1981.
  - when: 1986–98
    what: >-
      *Eros the Bittersweet* (1986), a long essay on desire in Greek lyric,
      lays the foundation for what follows. Then *Short Talks* (1992),
      *Glass, Irony, and God* (1995), with “The Glass Essay”, and
      *Autobiography of Red* (1998), the book that made her name.
  - when: 2001–10

  - when: "2026"

checks:
  - tempting: “A classicist who writes poems about Greek myths.”
    accurate: >-
      She is a classicist, and the Greeks are everywhere in her work. But the
      prize is for “new forms”. *Autobiography of Red* is a novel in verse.
      *The Beauty of the Husband* is “a fictional essay in 29 tangos”. “The
      Glass Essay” is a long poem that is also a story and a reading of Emily
      Brontë. *Nox* is a box. The committee says she has “broken away from
      lyrical poetry bound to the narrative self”, and that her writing moves
      “between different genres and artforms”. Calling her a poet is not
      wrong, but it is too small.
  - tempting: “She makes the classics relevant to modern readers.”
    accurate: >-
      She rejects the idea. Asked by the Nobel interviewer why the old stories
      are relevant today, she said: “It isn’t. I don’t think I’m interested in
      relevance. I’m interested in us being relevant to them.” Her own word
      for her authorship is “Inadvertent”. Let her keep the direction she chose.
  - tempting: >-
      “*Glass, Irony, and God*: a religious poet.” Or the reverse: “A
      secular modernist.”
    accurate: >-
      God comes into her titles and her subjects. *Decreation* has at its
      centre an essay on how Sappho, the medieval mystic Marguerite Porete and
      Simone Weil “tell God”, and she wrote the libretto for an opera installation,
      *The Mirror of Simple Souls*. But she writes about these women
      as a reader and a poet, and none of the prize reporting describes her
      own beliefs. CNN calls her “notoriously private”. Do not claim her for
      the faith, or against it.
  - tempting: “A good pick for the church book group.”
    accurate: >-
      She is often funny and her sentences are plain, but the books are not
      tame. In *Autobiography of Red* the young Geryon has, in the committee’s
      words, been “subjected to sexual assaults at the hands of his brother”
      before he falls in love with Heracles. *The Beauty of the Husband* is
      the story of a marriage going bad. “The Glass Essay” is shorter, and was
      Anders Olsson’s first choice. Read before you recommend.
  - tempting: “The first Canadian to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.”
    accurate: >-
      She is the third Canadian-born laureate, after Saul Bellow (1976), who
      moved to the United States as a child, and Alice Munro (2013). She
      holds Canadian and Icelandic citizenship and divides her time between
      Reykjavík and Ann Arbor, Michigan. She is the nineteenth woman among
      the 123 literature laureates.

    kind: instance
    refs: ["2 Corinthians 9:6–7", "Acts 20:35"]
    body: >-
      Minutes after the call, the Nobel interviewer asked her what the prize
      meant to her. She said she had not thought about it: “We’re just
      thinking of ways to give the money away. We have a friend who needs a
      house, and we always give a lot to Doctors Without Borders.” Paul
      writes: “Let each man give according as he has determined in his heart,
      not grudgingly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver”
      (2 Corinthians 9:7, WEB). The money had not yet arrived and the gift had
      not yet been made; what she describes is the deciding, which is what the
      verse names. These are her own words on the telephone, and she offered
      them without ceremony.
  - title: Living within the word “degenerative”
    kind: instance
    refs: ["2 Corinthians 4:16–18", "Psalm 71:9", "Psalm 71:18"]
    body: >-
      In 2024 she wrote in the London Review of Books that she has Parkinson’s
      disease, and that her handwriting had begun to come apart. “It is hard
      to live within the word ‘degenerative’, which means that, however I
      strive, I do not win.” Paul writes, “though our outward person is
      decaying, yet our inward person is renewed day by day” (2 Corinthians
      4:16, WEB). Her essay is a true case of the first half of that verse,
      told with exactness by someone who lives it. The second half is Paul’s
      claim and the preacher’s; she does not make it, and her words should
      not be bent to make it for her.
  - title: Relevant to the ancients
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["Jeremiah 6:16", "Hebrews 12:1"]
    body: >-
      Asked why Greek stories matter today, she turned the question round:
      “I’m interested in us being relevant to them.” When asked what inspires
      her, she said one word: “Homer.” Jeremiah tells the people to “ask for
      the old paths, ‘Where is the good way?’ and walk in it” (Jeremiah 6:16,
      WEB), and Hebrews sets the living before a great cloud of witnesses.
      The likeness is in the direction of the question: the old voices judge
      us, not we them. It fails at the point that matters. Her ancients are
      pagan poets, and she argues and plays with them; the prophet’s old path
      is the Lord’s way, given to be walked in.
  - title: The writer who would disappear
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["John 3:30", "Galatians 2:20", "Philippians 2:5–8"]
    body: >-
      Olsson says she “has always dreamt of a work where the self of the
      writer has disappeared”. In *Decreation* she takes up Simone Weil’s
      word for undoing the self, and states the snag: “I cannot go towards
      God…without bringing myself along.” John the Baptist says, “He must
      increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30, WEB). The image of a voice
      that wants to get out of the way of what it serves will preach. But
      John decreases so that a person may be seen, and Paul, who says that Christ
      lives in him (Galatians 2:20), is still unmistakably Paul. Carson’s question is about writing, and she
      leaves it as a paradox.
  - title: A brother who died far away
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["2 Samuel 18:33", "2 Samuel 12:23", "1 Thessalonians 4:13–14"]
    body: >-
      *Nox* is Carson’s epitaph for her brother Michael, estranged and long
      absent, who died in Denmark in 2000. Into it she put photographs,
      letters and her translation of the Roman poet Catullus’s elegy for his
      own brother, who also died on foreign soil. David weeps over the son who
      had turned against him: “My son Absalom! My son, my son Absalom!”
      (2 Samuel 18:33, WEB). Many in a congregation have mourned someone they
      no longer knew, and the book and the story speak to them alike. Where
      it fails: at the death of his other son David also says, “I will go to
      him” (2 Samuel 12:23, WEB). The committee calls *Nox* an epitaph, and a
      sermon should not read that promise into it.

shelf:
  - title: Journal
    note: 12 August 1748; 5 September 1769
    body: >-
      Asked for her sources of inspiration, Carson named Homer. Wesley read him on
      horseback. Riding to Newcastle in 1748 he finished the tenth book of the
      *Iliad*: *“What an amazing genius had this man! To write with such
      strength of thought, and beauty of expression, when he had none to go
      before him! And what a vein of piety runs through his whole work, in
      spite of his pagan prejudices!”* He was shocked by Zeus addressing his
      wife *“with such language as a carman might be ashamed of”*. In 1769 he
      read the *Odyssey* as he rode and found he had underrated it: *“Was ever
      man so happy in his descriptions, so exact and consistent in his
      characters, and so natural in telling a story?”* But he could not
      forgive the hero, who *“on the slightest pretence, tells deliberate
      lies over and over; nay, and is highly commended for so doing, even by
      the Goddess of Wisdom!”* Wesley read Homer as a moralist; Carson, in
      *Autobiography of Red*, takes the monster’s side against a smug hero.
      Both read closely and argued with the old poet, which is the habit a
      preacher can learn from either.
  - title: An Address to the Clergy
    note: London, 6 February 1756
    body: >-
      Carson’s life’s work has been reading Greek into English. Wesley asked
      every minister to examine himself on the same skill: *“Do I understand
      Greek and Hebrew? Otherwise, how can I undertake, (as every Minister
      does,) not only to explain books which are written therein, but to
      defend them against all opponents? Am I not at the mercy of every one
      who does understand, or even pretends to understand, the original?”*
      And more pointedly: *“Can I read into English one of David’s Psalms; or
      even the first chapter of Genesis?”* (§II.1). He also counted
      knowledge of *“profane history”* and *“ancient customs”* useful for
      understanding Scripture (§I.2). Her translations are a poet’s, not a
      preacher’s, but the slow work with an old language is where both begin.
  - title: On Mourning for the Dead
    note: sermon on 2 Samuel 12:23
    body: >-
      An early sermon for a young friend, set on David’s words at the death
      of his child. Wesley declines the flattering funeral eulogy of his day,
      which in his phrase described *“the beauty, not the likeness, of the
      picture”*: *“What he was, I am both unable to paint in suitable colours,
      and unwilling to attempt it.”* He speaks of his friend instead *“in a
      few plain words, such as were his own”*. He allows tears: *“At such a
      loss, if considered without the alleviating circumstances, who can
      blame him that drops a tear?”* But the young Wesley also speaks of *“the
      sinful nature, of profuse sorrowing for the dead”*, more sternly than
      most pastors would now. Read beside *Nox*, a book about not knowing the
      dead, it gives a preacher a model of honest restraint, and the comfort
      Wesley takes from David’s words: *“I shall go to him”*.

Anne Carson is a Canadian poet, essayist and translator, born in Toronto in 1950. She trained as a classicist and has taught classics, translation and artistic collaboration at universities in Canada and the United States. She now divides her time between Reykjavík and Ann Arbor, Michigan. The committee says almost all her work stands where “the philologist’s obsession with words” meets “the poet’s desire for new meaning”, and that she has named the history of words as the starting point for everything she writes.

Her method is to set an ancient voice beside a modern one and let them talk. In *Economy of the Unlost* the Greek poet Simonides sits beside Paul Celan; in *Men in the Off Hours*, Thucydides beside Virginia Woolf. Her best-known book, *Autobiography of Red* (1998), starts from fragments of the Greek poet Stesichoros about Geryon, a red winged monster killed by Heracles. Carson makes Geryon a shy boy, devoted to his mother, who falls in love with a boorish Heracles. The committee asks the book’s question plainly: “What happens when you love someone who will ultimately destroy you?”

She will not stay in one form. “The Glass Essay” is a long poem, a story of a woman left by her husband, and a reading of Emily Brontë. *The Beauty of the Husband* tells a failing marriage in twenty-nine tangos, each opening with lines from Keats. *Nox* (2010), for her brother Michael, is a box holding a long folded strip of photographs, letters and her translation of Catullus’s elegy for his brother. She has also made plays, opera texts and performances, and translated Sappho, Sophocles, Aeschylus and Euripides in versions the committee calls “independent works in their own right”. She writes in English, so the books below are her own, with three of her translations among them.

She is witty and her sentences are often plain, but she can be hard to follow, because she jumps between voices and centuries without warning. Asked where a newcomer should begin, she told the Nobel interviewer: “Start in the middle, I always say.” For a pastor, a good way in is “The Glass Essay”, in *Glass, Irony, and God*, which was Anders Olsson’s first choice; *Autobiography of Red* and *Nox* are his other two.
