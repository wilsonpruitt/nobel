checks:
  - tempting: >-
      “The prize is a verdict on Gaza.” Or the reverse: “The prize proves
      the Gaza report was wrong-headed and political.”
    accurate: >-
      The committee lists her chairing of the UN commission of inquiry on the
      Occupied Palestinian Territory among her posts, in one sentence, and
      says nothing about its findings. In 2025 that commission concluded that
      Israel was responsible for genocide in Gaza. Israel dismissed the report
      as “scandalous”, and on the day of the prize its Foreign Ministry called
      the award “the grotesque weaponisation of an award to legitimise
      anti-Israel hatred and prejudice”. Pillay, for her part, asked: “Why
      would you need courage to speak the truth?” A commission of inquiry is
      not a court, and its conclusions are not a judgment. Neither the prize
      nor this page settles the question, and a sermon should not try to.
  - tempting: “She is a judge of the World Court.”
    accurate: >-
      Not quite. The International Court of Justice has its own elected
      judges. In *The Gambia v. Myanmar* the court’s orders list her
      separately, as one of two judges ad hoc added for that case alone. The
      case is still running. The committee’s words are that Myanmar “stands
      accused” of genocide; no verdict has been given, and nothing here
      should suggest one. Keep the three courts apart, too: the Rwanda
      tribunal (a UN court set up for one genocide), the International
      Criminal Court (which tries individuals), and the International Court
      of Justice (which hears disputes between states).
  - tempting: “She made rape a crime of genocide.”
    accurate: >-
      Not alone. Akayesu, the mayor of Taba, was tried before three judges,
      with Laïty Kama presiding and Lennart Aspegren and Pillay beside him.
      The rape charges came into the case because a prosecution lawyer found
      the evidence and the indictment was amended, and above all because
      Rwandan women testified, from behind a curtain, to what had been done
      to them. The committee says Pillay’s influence was “a significant
      factor”. The judgment also convicted Akayesu of direct and public
      incitement to commit genocide, for speeches he made at a public meeting
      in April 1994. Credit her fully, and credit the witnesses first.
  - tempting: >-
      “The committee was rebuking Trump.” Or: “The prize is anti-American.”
    accurate: >-
      The committee named no government. It said that international law is
      “under tremendous pressure”, that its institutions are “under attack”,
      and that its judges are being sanctioned. The United States has
      sanctioned ICC officials and, on the day of the prize, the court
      itself; Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the court a “threat to
      U.S. sovereignty”, and the United States has never joined it. President
      Trump said the committee had “demeaned” the prize. Pillay joked, “If
      you clap a little bit more, I may share this prize with Trump.” Report
      what was said and done. The prize is about the rule of law, and a
      sermon on it need not be about Washington.
  - tempting: “A figure everyone admires.”
    accurate: >-
      She is widely honoured: the UN Secretary-General, the President of the
      ICC and South Africa’s President all praised the award. But her work
      has drawn fire before. As High Commissioner she called in 2009 for an
      investigation of both sides in Sri Lanka’s civil war, and Sri Lanka’s
      human rights minister objected that her statements made dialogue
      harder. In 2010 she was criticised for saying
      she would not attend the Nobel ceremony for the Chinese dissident Liu
      Xiaobo; her office denied bowing to Chinese pressure, cited a clash with
      a Human Rights Day event, and she called for his release. The
      committee itself concedes that international law has “produced
      different results depending on who is the perpetrator and who is the
      victim”.

preaching:
  - title: The small and the great alike
    kind: instance
    refs: ["Deuteronomy 1:16–17", "Proverbs 31:8–9"]
    body: >-
      Moses charged Israel’s judges: “You shall not show partiality in
      judgment; you shall hear the small and the great alike. You shall not
      be afraid of the face of man, for the judgment is God’s” (Deuteronomy
      1:17, WEB). In Arusha in 1997 and 1998 three judges heard Rwandan women,
      sitting behind a curtain, give evidence against the man who
      had been their mayor. Their testimony made rape part of the first
      conviction for genocide by an international court. The tribunal was not
      Israel’s court and claimed no word from God, but what Moses demands of
      a judge is what happened there: the small were heard against the
      great.
  - title: As bound with them
    kind: instance
    refs: ["Hebrews 13:3", "Matthew 25:36"]
    body: >-
      “Remember those who are in bonds, as bound with them” (Hebrews 13:3,
      WEB). Under apartheid Pillay defended detainees and, when her own
      husband was held under the Terrorism Act, defended him too; in 1973 she
      won for the political prisoners on Robben Island the right to see a
      lawyer. Hebrews is written to the church, and nothing here says she
      acted from it. But the remembering the verse asks for, by someone who
      shares the danger, is what she did.
  - title: Swords into plowshares
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["Isaiah 2:2–4", "Micah 4:1–4"]
    body: >-
      “He will judge between the nations, and will decide concerning many
      peoples. They shall beat their swords into plowshares” (Isaiah 2:4,
      WEB). The committee’s hope, that conflicts be settled by courts rather
      than by force, borrows the shape of the prophet’s vision. Use the image
      and no more. In Isaiah the judge is the Lord, the law goes out from
      Zion, and the nations come to learn his ways. Human courts are slow,
      their reach is partial, and their rulings are disputed; The Hague is
      not the mountain of the Lord.
  - title: The work of righteousness
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["Isaiah 32:15–17", "Psalm 85:10"]
    body: >-
      “Peace requires justice,” says the committee. Isaiah had said it first:
      “The work of righteousness will be peace” (Isaiah 32:17, WEB). The two
      sentences can stand side by side in a sermon. But Isaiah’s justice
      comes “until the Spirit is poured on us from on high” (32:15, WEB): it
      is God’s gift, and the peace that follows is his. The committee’s
      justice is built by treaties, courts and judges. A preacher can honour
      that work without pretending it is the same thing.

Navi Pillay is a South African judge, born in Durban in 1941 to a family of Indian Tamil origin. She grew up under apartheid, became a lawyer, and in 1967 opened her own practice, where she defended people the apartheid state had detained. After apartheid ended she became a judge, first in South Africa and then on the international courts set up to try the worst crimes there are: genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes. She has since served as the UN’s High Commissioner for Human Rights, chaired the UN commission of inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory, and sits as a judge in the case at the International Court of Justice in which Myanmar is accused of genocide against the Rohingya.

Her best-known work was done at the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda, which the UN set up after the genocide of 1994. In 1998 that tribunal gave the first conviction for genocide by an international court, against Jean-Paul Akayesu, the mayor of Taba. Pillay was one of the three judges. Rwandan women had testified to rapes that Akayesu used his authority to encourage, and the judgment held that rape and sexual violence, done to destroy a people, can be acts of genocide. It was the first time an international court had defined rape as a crime under international law. Pillay said afterwards that the tribunal wanted to “send out a strong message that rape is no longer a trophy of war.”

The Peace Prize is different from the other Nobels. It is awarded by the Norwegian Nobel Committee and presented in Oslo, not Stockholm, and it honours work for peace rather than a discovery. The committee ties this year’s award to Nobel’s own will: by advancing the use of tribunals for peace and justice, it says, she “strengthens fraternity between nations”. Its argument is that lasting peace depends on settling conflicts by law rather than by force, and that this system is now under strain, with more wars and its judges sanctioned. It is candid about the system’s flaws: the greatest powers, it says, “have often evaded responsibility.”

The prize is controversial. Israel’s government condemned it on the day, because of the Gaza inquiry Pillay chaired, and the White House announced new sanctions on the International Criminal Court the same day. A pastor should know these things and report them fairly, but this page takes no side in cases still before the courts. What will preach is older than any of them: judges who will not be afraid of the face of man, victims who are heard, and the hope that nations will one day settle their quarrels by justice instead of by the sword.
