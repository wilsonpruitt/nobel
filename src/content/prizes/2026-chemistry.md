  - tempting: “They made chirality out of nothing.”
    accurate: >-
      A committee member did say “from nothing”, meaning from starting

      the flask. What came from nowhere was only a preference: a random,
      minuscule imbalance between the two hands, which the reaction
      then multiplied.
  - tempting: “Thalidomide was the wrong mirror image, so pure drugs would have prevented the disaster.”
    accurate: >-
      The Nobel material tells the story that way, and it is why chemists
      care so much about making one hand only. The fuller picture is less
      tidy. The two forms of thalidomide turn into each other in the body,
      and chemists writing in *Scientific Reports* in 2018 say it is uncertain
      whether any of its effects could have been avoided by giving a pure
      form. Use thalidomide as a reason for care, not as a clean proof.
  - tempting: “The Soai reaction is how our medicines are made.”
    accurate: >-
      The Soai reaction works for only a narrow group of molecules. What
      chemists use every day is Kagan’s insight, as a way of understanding and
      improving the reactions that make one-handed drugs, flavours and scents.
      A committee member said it was difficult to name a drug that owes
      something to it: “I would say all of them, because we use this as
      a tool.”

Many molecules come in two forms that are mirror images of each other, like a left and a right hand. They are built from the same atoms joined in the same order, but one cannot be laid exactly over the other. Living things are strict about this. The amino acids in your proteins are all one hand, and the sugars in your DNA are all one hand too. Yet when chemists make such molecules in a flask, they almost always get an even mix of both. This matters for medicine, because the body is one-handed and the two forms of a drug can act quite differently in it.

In 1953 the physicist Charles Frank worked out on paper how a single hand could win out. He needed a molecule that helps make more of itself, and some way for each hand to hold the other back. Henri Kagan supplied the second part in 1986. Chemists then used catalysts made partly of a one-handed molecule, and assumed that a less pure catalyst would simply give a less pure product. Kagan reasoned that the catalyst’s metal holds at least two of these molecules at once, so a mixed catalyst could pair a left with a right, and that this odd pair might work slowly. He was right. The matched pairs did the work, and the product came out purer than the catalyst.

Kenso Soai supplied the first part. In 1995 he published a reaction whose product is itself the catalyst for making more of the same product, and it amplifies whichever hand is ahead. By 2003 he had a reaction in which a tiny chance excess of one hand takes over almost the whole product. Run it again and the other hand may win. Nothing of the kind had been done outside living things. It shows that one-handedness can arise without help from life, but it is a laboratory reaction, not a reconstruction of how life began.
