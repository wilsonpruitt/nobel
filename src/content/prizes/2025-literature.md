timeline:
  - when: 1954–1980s
    what: >-
      Born on 5 January 1954 in Gyula, a small town in south-east Hungary near
      the Romanian border. He studies law in Szeged and Budapest, then spends
      years in poor villages and bad jobs: a spell in a mine, night watchman
      for three hundred cows, director of village culture houses. After he
      tells the police he does not write about politics, they take his
      passport. He has none until 1987.
  - when: 1985–89
    what: >-
      *Satantango* (1985) is a sensation in Hungary. *The Melancholy of
      Resistance* follows in 1989. Susan Sontag calls him the “master of the
      apocalypse”.
  - when: 1994–2011
    what: >-
      Béla Tarr films *Satantango* (1994) and, from *The Melancholy of
      Resistance*, *Werckmeister Harmonies* (2000). The two write the
      screenplays together, through *The Turin Horse* (2011). *Seiobo There
      Below* appears in Hungarian in 2008.
  - when: 2012–24
    what: >-
      New Directions publishes the English translations that make his name
      in America. He wins the Man Booker International Prize in 2015 and the
      US National Book Award for translated literature in 2019. *Herscht
      07769* (2021) reaches English in 2024.
  - when: "2025"
    what: >-
      The prize, announced on 9 October. He hears the news at a sick friend’s
      flat in Frankfurt and tells the Nobel interviewer: “This is more than a
      catastrophe”, borrowing Beckett’s joke, then adds that it is “happiness
      and proudness”. Nobel lecture in Stockholm on 7 December; banquet speech
      on 10 December.
  - when: "2026"
    what: >-
      Béla Tarr dies; his funeral is held in Budapest on 6 February. The
      Nobel lecture is published in English as *Enough About Angels* in
      April. On 8 September Germany awards him the Grand Cross of its Order
      of Merit; he has held dual German and Hungarian citizenship since 2017.

checks:
  - tempting: >-
      “A Christian novelist.” Or the reverse: “A writer who has given up on
      God.”
    accurate: >-
      Neither label is his. In 2021 he said that “faith itself has come to an
      end”, and in the same answer that when the noose tightens “you cling to
      the Lord God with two hands, three even”. In 2012 he explained his long
      sentences this way: “Only God needs the period—and at the end He will
      use one, I am sure.” His Nobel banquet speech was a list of thanks that
      ran from a Gyula church sexton and cobbler, through Kafka, Kyoto,
      “Johann Sebastian Bach, for the Divine” and Prince Siddhartha, and ended
      “To God.” In the Nobel lecture he speaks of “our souls which are
      incapable of belief”. He uses the language of faith often and professes
      none. Let him speak for himself.
  - tempting: “His books are pure despair.”
    accurate: >-
      They are bleak, and he says bitterness is his deepest inspiration. But
      the committee lists “pessimism and apocalypse, but also humour and
      unpredictability”, and the citation itself says his work “reaffirms the
      power of art”. Presenting the prize, Anders Olsson said that hope does
      not appear to be lost in this dark world: “It is just that we do not
      know how or when it will emerge.” A Hungarian scholar quoted by the
      Associated Press called the novels “incredibly funny”. When the motto
      of *Herscht 07769* (“Hope is an error”) was put to him, he explained it
      through the physics of the universe’s first moments and concluded that
      “it really means that the world was born out of hope.”
  - tempting: “A Nobel winner, so a good pick for the church book group.”
    accurate: >-
      He is hard going, and admirers say so. His sentences can run for pages;
      *Herscht 07769* has one full stop in about four hundred. A Budapest
      bookseller called him “an exceptionally demanding and sophisticated
      author”. The prize honours achievement, not ease. A reader who wants to
      try him should start with *Seiobo There Below*, read slowly, and expect
      to be disoriented.
  - tempting: >-
      “The prize was a rebuke to Orbán.” Or: “He is a political dissident.”
    accurate: >-
      He has criticised Viktor Orbán’s government, especially over its stance
      on Ukraine, but he told a Swedish paper that “There is no hope left in
      Hungary today and it is not only because of the Orbán regime”: the
      problem, he said, is social as well as political. Orbán congratulated
      him publicly. Under communism the secret police thought him an
      anticommunist, but he says he made no political demonstrations and told
      them he did not write about contemporary politics. The committee’s
      citation is about art. Do not make the prize a verdict on Hungary.
  - tempting: >-
      “*Satantango* is about the devil,” or “his apocalypse is the Book of
      Revelation.”
    accurate: >-
      In *Satantango* two men believed dead return to a ruined collective
      farm. To the villagers they seem messengers “either of hope or of the
      last judgement”, and in Olsson’s words “it is impossible to know
      which”. The committee finds the satanic element in a con man’s
      deceptions and in the villagers’ “slave morality”. His apocalypse is
      terror and disorder in small towns, with no judge and no promised end.
      Biblical imagery is everywhere, but it is not a key to the books.

preaching:
  - title: The man on the platform
    kind: instance
    refs: ["James 2:1–6", "Luke 10:30–37", "Matthew 25:35–40"]
    body: >-
      In the Nobel lecture Krasznahorkai described something he saw in Berlin
      in the early 1990s. An old homeless man, in pain, was trying to relieve
      himself beyond the yellow line at the end of a U-Bahn platform, his
      face turned towards the crowd “like someone who counted on sympathy”.
      The crowd decided at once that this was a scandal that must end, and a
      policeman ran to catch him. James rebukes a church that has favoured the
      well dressed: “But you have dishonored the poor man” (James 2:6, WEB).
      The crowd in Berlin did what James describes. The lecture is the novelist’s own
      account, shaped as he tells it; but he tells it as something that
      happened.
  - title: Going down to the poor
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["Ezekiel 3:15", "Hebrews 13:3"]
    body: >-
      Ezekiel went to the exiles by the river Chebar and sat among them,
      overwhelmed, for seven days before the word came to him. Krasznahorkai, in his twenties, went to live
      among the poorest people in Hungarian villages, “because of my very
      huge impression of Dostoyevsky”, and stayed for years; *Satantango*
      came out of that life. He was no prophet and claimed no word from God,
      so his story is a likeness of Ezekiel’s, not a case of it. But it shows
      the shape of the thing: go where the afflicted are, and stay long
      enough to see what they see before saying a word about them.
  - title: Angels in street clothes
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["Hebrews 13:2", "Genesis 18:1–8"]
    body: >-
      The Nobel lecture opens with his announcement that he had meant to talk
      about hope, “but as my stores of hope have definitively come to an end,
      I will now speak about angels” (trans. Ottilie Mulzet). His new angels
      have no wings and no message; “they walk among us in simple street
      clothes”. “Don’t forget to show hospitality to strangers, for in doing
      so, some have entertained angels without knowing it” (Hebrews 13:2,
      WEB). The image will preach. His angels, though, bring nothing from God
      and are wounded by us; Scripture’s strangers come to Abraham’s tent
      with a promise.
  - title: Hope that is not seen
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["Romans 8:24–25", "Lamentations 3:19–24", "Ecclesiastes 1:2–11"]
    body: >-
      The lecture begins by saying his hope has run out and ends, after
      angels, human dignity and the Berlin platform, by saying he has spoken
      “maybe about everything—even hope.” Lamentations makes a similar turn
      after chapters of ruin: “This I recall to my mind; therefore I have
      hope” (Lamentations 3:21, WEB). The likeness lies in the turn, which a
      sermon can borrow. The grounds differ. The poet of Lamentations hopes
      because of the Lord’s loving kindnesses; Krasznahorkai names no ground.
      Paul’s “hope that is seen is not hope” (Romans 8:24, WEB) may speak to
      readers who find his bleakness more honest than easy cheer.
  - title: Those who carry the work and do not understand it
    kind: likeness
    refs: ["1 Peter 1:10–12", "1 Corinthians 3:6–9"]
    body: >-
      In *Seiobo There Below* the great artists are mostly offstage. The
      committee says we meet instead “janitors, onlookers or devoted
      craftsmen, who rarely or indeed never comprehend the meaning of the
      work in which they participate”: drunken assistants carrying an
      unfinished Perugino to Perugia, an old craftsman carving Noh masks. Peter says
      the prophets served a salvation they searched for and did not see, and
      that angels long to look into it. Most of a congregation’s work is like
      that. The book is fiction and proves nothing, but its picture of
      faithful labour on a whole one cannot see is true to church life.

shelf:
  - title: Letters on *The Fool of Quality*
    note: to Henry Brooke, Hull, 8 July 1774; to Mary Bishop, Haverfordwest, 18 August 1784
    body: >-
      Wesley read novels and judged them by what they did to the reader. Of
      Henry Brooke’s *The Fool of Quality* he wrote to a younger Henry
      Brooke, not the author, that he *“could not but observe the design of it, to promote the
      religion of the heart”*. He offered to send the author corrections to
      the *“structure of the story”*, and later abridged the novel for his
      own people. Ten years on he was cautious: *“I would recommend very few
      novels to young persons, for fear they should be too desirous of
      more.”* He preferred true accounts. After reading Commodore Byron’s
      account of his hardships in his scraps of time, he wrote: *“Sure no Novel in the
      world can be more affecting, or more surprising, than this history”*
      (Journal, 30 March 1769). Krasznahorkai’s books are not built to
      promote anything, and Wesley would have asked what they do to the
      soul. That is a fair question for a preacher to put to them too.
  - title: Heaviness through Manifold Temptations
    note: sermon on 1 Peter 1:6
    body: >-
      Wesley would have recognised Krasznahorkai’s villages. Poverty, he
      says, *“may appear but a small thing to those who stand at a distance,
      or who look, and ‘pass by on the other side;’ yet it is otherwise to
      them who feel it”*, and he cries out: *“O want of bread! Want of bread!
      Who can tell what this means unless he hath felt it himself?”*
      (§III.3). He allows the believer deep and lasting sorrow. What he will
      not allow is despair as a settled state: *“there may be need of
      heaviness, but there can be no need of darkness”* (§V.2). Here he and
      the novelist part company, and a sermon should say so.
  - title: The General Deliverance
    note: sermon on Romans 8:19–22
    body: >-
      Krasznahorkai’s apocalypse has terror and no judge. Wesley’s end of the
      world is the deliverance of the whole creation, animals included. The
      creatures’ groans, he says, *“are not dispersed in idle air, but enter
      into the ears of Him that made them”*, and they will be set free *“(not
      by annihilation; annihilation is not deliverance)”* (§III.1). The
      novelist’s banquet speech thanked “the last wolf in Extremadura” and
      “nature, that was created”. Read beside his bleakest pages, this sermon
      shows a preacher how to take the terror seriously without granting
      that it has the last word.

László Krasznahorkai is a Hungarian novelist, born in 1954 in Gyula, a small town near the Romanian border. He lives near Budapest. His novels are set in places that are falling apart: a ruined collective farm in endless rain, a town in a Carpathian valley where a circus arrives with the carcass of a giant whale and riot follows, a town in eastern Germany where neo-Nazis and arson invade a quiet life. Con men are taken for saviours. Holy fools put their trust in the wrong people. Susan Sontag called him the “master of the apocalypse”. The committee places him in a Central European line that runs through Kafka to Thomas Bernhard, with absurdity and grotesque excess, but also with humour.

What a reader notices first is the sentences. They wind on for pages without a full stop, building, doubling back, gathering speed. He says they come from speech: people who badly need to say something do not talk in short sentences. The effect is close to music, and it is hard work; you cannot skim him. Yet beauty keeps breaking in. Bach’s music sounds through *Herscht 07769*. In *Seiobo There Below* (2008) a white heron stands motionless in a Kyoto river while crowds pass without seeing it, and seventeen stories follow the making of art, from a Noh mask to a Renaissance painting, in a world the committee calls one “of blindness and impermanence”. One begins: “He already knew how to draw a Madonna even before he knew what a Madonna was” (trans. Ottilie Mulzet).

In English he has three main translators, George Szirtes, Ottilie Mulzet and John Batki, and New Directions is his American publisher; the main books are listed below. He also wrote the screenplays for Béla Tarr’s films with Tarr, among them *Satantango* (1994), *Werckmeister Harmonies* (2000) and *The Turin Horse* (2011).

He is hard going, and nothing on this page should suggest otherwise. A pastor who has never read him should start with *Seiobo There Below*. It was the choice of Anders Olsson, who chairs the Nobel Committee, and a reviewer for NPR called it “brighter and more open” than the earlier novels and “an excellent introduction” to fiction that is “difficult but deeply rewarding”. Its chapters connect only loosely, so they can be read one at a time. Read slowly, and aloud if the sentences lose you.
